Analytical Philosophy

How I Think

The principles behind every analysis I do — from raw data to decision-ready insight.

🎯
Start with the Decision, Not the Data
Before touching a dataset, I ask: what decision does this analysis need to support? That shapes everything — which metrics matter, how precise we need to be, and when "good enough" is better than "perfect."
⚖️
Embrace Analytical Trade-offs
Real analysis involves trade-offs. A well-reasoned proxy metric that gets us to insight faster can beat a technically perfect measure that arrives too late. Judgement — knowing when to simplify — is part of the skill.
📖
Transparency Over Polish
I document assumptions, limitations, and the "why" behind every major analytical choice. A stakeholder should be able to trace how I got from raw data to conclusion — and challenge any step along the way.
🧭
Drive Toward a Conclusion
It's easy to get lost in data. I keep the higher-level goal in sight and always land on a clear takeaway — even if that takeaway is "we need more information before we can decide."
🗣️
Translate Numbers into Stories
A model is only as valuable as the decision it enables. I focus on translating outputs into plain language: what makes this number good or bad, why did it change, and what should we do about it.
🔍
Own What I Know (and Don't)
I only present work I understand deeply — including the alternatives I considered and why I made the choices I did. If I can't explain a decision clearly, I go back and re-examine it before presenting.

"The best analysts weigh the benefits and costs to get the right insight for the decision at hand — not just the most technically correct one."

A principle I try to embody in every project

Healthcare · Predictive Modeling
Hospital Utilization Predictive Analysis
Team project (4 members) · Heritage Health Prize data
End-to-end analysis of 71,435 health plan members over 3 years across four phases: EDA, data preparation, NLP, and predictive modeling of Y3 hospitalization from Y1–Y2 behavioral and clinical features.
🎯 Key Insight

Just 4.3% of members drive disproportionate hospital costs, and Y2 high utilizers had a 2.5× higher Y3 hospitalization rate. With 85% of members at zero hospital days, early identification of the few high-risk members is where proactive care management pays off.

⚖️ Analytical Trade-off

Kept the target strictly in the future (Y1–Y2 features predicting Y3), so the model mirrors how a care team would actually use it: flagging members before the costly year, not explaining it afterward.

NLP · Finance · Risk Analytics
Quantifying Corporate Risk via Text Analytics
Scraped 10-K Item 1A (Risk Factors) filings for 10 S&P 500 firms (2015–2024) via SEC EDGAR, tokenized 231,288 tokens across 50 firm-year documents, applied LDA (K=6), and regressed textual uncertainty on next-year stock volatility.
🎯 Key Insight

LDA surfaced six risk themes, from regulatory to energy/commodity. Uncertainty language pointed toward higher next-year volatility (β = 2.49), but the effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.464, R² = 0.014), so text-based risk screening works best as a complement to traditional financial metrics, not a replacement.

⚖️ Analytical Trade-off

Used the Loughran-McDonald finance-specific lexicon over a general sentiment dictionary, trading broader coverage for domain accuracy, since general lexicons misread many routine finance terms as negative.

Marketing Analytics · Segmentation
Customer Segmentation — Cluster Analysis
Segmented 278 online pickup customers into 3 distinct clusters using hierarchical and k-means clustering on age, income, spending score, and gender.
🎯 Key Insight

High income did not mean high spending: the two showed up as distinct segments. That split points to two different plays — loyalty programs for high spenders and upsell campaigns for high-income, low-spend customers.

⚖️ Analytical Trade-off

Segmented on spending behavior alongside demographics rather than income alone, because a purely demographic split would have merged two groups that need very different marketing.

Customer Analytics · Retention
Streaming Service Churn — Survival Analysis
Modeled subscriber churn with Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox Proportional Hazards, comparing users past a 1,000-hour engagement milestone with promotion-driven users.
🎯 Key Insight

Customers past 1,000 hours of engagement were retained significantly longer (log-rank confirmed), while promotions showed a near-zero long-term retention effect. Discounts attract but don't retain; building engagement habits early does.

⚖️ Analytical Trade-off

Chose survival analysis over logistic regression, because survival models handle time-to-event data and still-active (censored) customers naturally, where a yes/no churn model would bias retention estimates.
